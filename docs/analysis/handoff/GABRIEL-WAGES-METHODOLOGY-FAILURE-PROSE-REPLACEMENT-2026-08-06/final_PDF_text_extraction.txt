PART VI · METHODOLOGY AND PROJECT HISTORY
How I built the project
I began with a simple empirical question: why might police and fire wages grow differently from the wages of other municipal employees? The useful comparison
was not a pile of safety contracts by itself. I needed safety and non-safety units from the same municipality and roughly the same compensation cycle so that
place and time were held as constant as the documents allowed. That matched design shaped the original collection strategy, but the workflow was not fully
specified at the beginning. I changed it as the evidence exposed weaknesses in each earlier approach.
The first stage was broad discovery. I built a universe of 35,589 eligible municipalities and recorded scout coverage for 35,574 of them. Coverage meant that a
municipality had been searched through the approved discovery process; it did not mean that every relevant document was found or that every city supplied a
usable comparison. Candidate sources then went through verification, source review, exact-duplicate checks, retention, and document-readiness review. I treated
those as separate decisions because a link can be discoverable but unavailable, a file can be retained but unreadable, and a readable document can still lack the
side, period, role, or pay basis needed for analysis.
The resulting corpus was large and mixed. It included 15,163 unique PDFs with 1,029,482 native PDF pages, as well as HTML pages, tables, embedded
structured records, and small tabular files. The later external collection produced 14,160 usable payloads. Those counts describe different units and were never
added into one master total. More importantly, I did not treat size as proof. A million pages can document many municipal processes while still failing to create a
clean safety-versus-non-safety wage panel.
Once files were ready, Codex recovered text, tables, embedded records, targeted fields, and exact source spans. Earlier documentary material received
GABRIEL ratings when it was eligible. The later administrative material was processed with deterministic local rules and bounded semantic AI review when
additional GABRIEL-supported processing was no longer available. Extraction was deliberately generous at first so that potentially relevant material would not be
lost too early. That choice produced millions of provisional records and made a separate compaction stage necessary.
Compaction reduced repeated, ambiguous, and structurally redundant material before analysis. The compact records were then reconciled across municipality,
department side, period, source identity, role, and compensation basis. Normalization made units more comparable without pretending that incompatible
measures were the same. Mathematical analysis tested only the comparisons that survived those gates. Source-level semantic review then returned to
claim-critical excerpts and tables to check whether the structured interpretation still matched the underlying language.
The final analytical design kept both a strict layer and broader, explicitly qualified layers. I used aggressive bounded reanalysis to recover evidence that was
useful with a clear caveat, but I did not overwrite the strict results. Counterexamples, conflicts, rejected records, and unsupported propositions remained visible
during final adjudication. That process ended with fourteen locked claims, each assigned one class, defensible wording, prohibited wording, evidence links,
limitations, and a report placement. Visual production came last. The figures summarize an adjudicated evidence system; they are not a substitute for it.

How I used AI and why relays mattered
I made the research decisions. I set the question, chose priorities, reviewed outputs, corrected the framing, challenged interpretations that were either too loose or
too restrictive, approved the evidence standards, and determined the final voice and presentation. I did not manually read or classify millions of records. My role
was to direct the research, inspect consequential outputs, decide what counted as defensible evidence, and make the final judgment calls.
ChatGPT translated those decisions into bounded stages. It wrote self-contained prompts for Codex, specified allowed and forbidden actions, designed validation
gates, developed the evidence-tier framework, reviewed relay packages, and planned the reporting and handoff structure. Codex performed the local execution: it
created and ran scripts, processed files, carried out authorized searches, built structured layers, saved checkpoints, ran validation, generated figures and PDFs,
and committed accepted outputs. GABRIEL supplied numerical and qualitative ratings for eligible earlier documentary evidence. When that scoring route was not
available for the later external administrative layer, deterministic rules and bounded semantic AI review handled the narrower classification work.
These roles were complementary, but they were not interchangeable. ChatGPT could design a strong validation plan without having executed the files. Codex
could produce millions of structured records without deciding what the project should claim. GABRIEL could score eligible text without settling the research
design. I used the systems to extend the amount of material that could be searched, organized, and checked, while retaining human responsibility for the
question, standards, interpretation, and final exclusions.
From a continuing log to relay packages
At first, the handoff between stages depended on a continuing project log called PROGRESS.md. Decisions, surprises, and next steps were copied forward
manually. That was workable while the project was small because one entry could summarize the relevant state. As the number of tasks, local artifacts, validation
reports, and partially completed processes grew, the log became cumbersome. Too much context had to be carried by hand, it became harder to tell which results
were final, and assumptions could drift between one run and the next.
I replaced that system with compact ZIP relay packages. At the end of a bounded Codex stage, the relay recorded what changed, which files were created or
modified, the commit, validation results, decisions, blockers, uncertainties, and the recommended next step. The relay did not attempt to copy the full corpus. It
preserved enough compact state to establish what had actually happened.
The working loop became consistent. Codex executed a bounded task and created a relay. I uploaded that relay to ChatGPT. ChatGPT reviewed the recorded
output, explained the result in plain language, and drafted the next self-contained prompt. Codex then resumed from the accepted repository state rather than
from an informal description of unfinished work. This reduced copy-and-paste context, strengthened traceability, made error recovery easier, and separated
planning from execution. It also made failures harder to hide: a stage that produced zero compatible matches, a deployment delay, or a partial worker result had
to be recorded before the next task could be designed around it.

How Codex execution evolved and scaled
The project used two broad execution profiles: Routine and Heavy. Routine work covered smaller deterministic rebuilds, maintenance, and validation. Heavy work
covered large, uncertain, architectural, or claim-critical stages where the execution plan itself required more judgment. The distinction was practical rather than
analytical: it helped match the amount of checking and orchestration to the risk of the stage.
I also experimented with Git worktrees as a way to isolate simultaneous tasks. Separate working directories looked attractive because workers could avoid direct
file collisions. In practice, the worktrees duplicated state, complicated branch and merge management, and made shared summaries harder to reconcile. For
many stages, the coordination burden exceeded the isolation benefit. I reduced their use and moved toward a staggered lane system inside one controlled project
state.
For large jobs, I stopped treating Codex as one worker moving through one enormous queue. Instead, a task was divided into independent lanes. Each lane
received a locked portion of the work, such as a set of states, sources, documents, claims, or figures, and wrote only to lane-owned files. Workers started a few
minutes apart so they were less likely to compete for memory, disk access, or an external service at exactly the same time. Each lane saved accepted progress
frequently. Some stages used small atomic checkpoints and append-only ledgers so completed records did not have to be rewritten every time state changed.
The resume rule was equally important: restart only incomplete work. A worker interruption was not a reason to throw away already accepted outputs. After the
lanes finished, one coordinator reconciled the counts, checked lineage and hashes, merged the lane outputs, and updated shared summaries. Shared files were
protected until that merge. This structure reduced full restarts and made large batches manageable, but it did not remove coordination costs. Workers still had to
be monitored, duplicate processes could appear, and partial results could disagree. The lane system is useful when work can be divided cleanly; it is not a general
reason to parallelize every task.
How the project scaled
The workflow grew through calibrated steps. A four-row GABRIEL pilot tested the first rating assumptions. A 150-source review pilot produced 149 downloads and
exposed practical source-handling issues. Later batches grew to 500 sources and then 1,500 sources. Broad scouting expanded through 4,000-row and
10,000-row stages before reaching the 35,589-municipality universe. The external processing layer eventually retained 14,160 usable payloads, and extraction
created millions of provisional records before compaction. The final analytical universe, however, remained fourteen claims.
The pilots mattered because they tested schemas, provenance, extraction behavior, validation, and output quality while errors were still cheap to repair. Once a
stage had passed calibration, repeating tiny pilots stopped adding much information. Larger resumable batches became more efficient, provided that the inputs
were locked, progress was checkpointed, and outputs could be audited independently. Scaling therefore meant more than making a batch larger. It meant
changing the state design so that a larger batch could fail without erasing everything that had already worked.

What failed during collection and processing
The collection and processing workflow did not proceed in a straight line. Several failures changed the method, and I preserved them because they explain the
limits of the final evidence.
The clearest discovery failure occurred when the later residual hosted-search channel began returning results without usable sources. Repeated attempts did not
establish that the remaining 12,844 targets lacked evidence. They established only that the search route had stopped producing verifiable material, possibly
because of an external product or capacity limit that the project could not diagnose conclusively. I therefore preserved those targets as unsearched discovery
gaps. Coding them as negative results would have confused a failed search channel with an absence of documents.
Storage also became a binding constraint. One retention stage reached roughly a 30 GiB local ceiling. The project retained 14,449 external payloads, while 7,895
verified sources remained held because of storage capacity. Their metadata and recovery status were preserved rather than silently discarded. Final claim
adjudication found that no held-source recovery was required for the present claims or visuals, but that conclusion does not make the held material irrelevant to
future work.
Some early checkpoint designs did not scale. Large arrays embedded inside state files became slow and memory intensive. Later stages replaced them with
smaller checkpoints, append-only ledgers, hashed queues, and compact summaries. That repair changed how the process recorded progress, not the accepted
evidence itself. It also reinforced the rule that a checkpoint should identify completed work rather than duplicate an entire analytical layer.
Documents and parsers created a different set of failures. Some files were corrupt, invalidly compressed, nearly empty, shell-like wrappers, or hostile to the
available parser. Long-running child processes needed wall-time limits and quarantine paths. OCR was deferred for 118 sources rather than run indiscriminately,
because this project had authorized a large non-OCR extraction stage and did not treat every low-text file as permission to introduce a new recognition pipeline.
Files that failed remained classified as repair, manual-review, or OCR-later cases instead of being forced into the usable corpus.
The most visible processing problem was extraction overproduction. Generous recovery produced 5,558,770 raw field hits and 4,289,437 raw spans. Many were
repeated table cells, boilerplate, ambiguous labels, or several representations of the same underlying fact. Raw extraction volume was therefore not evidence
volume. Deterministic compaction reduced the material to 1,876,183 compact administrative observations while preserving pointers back to the source layers.
The large reduction was a correction to representation, not the deletion of inconvenient findings.
An early emphasis on wage tables was also too narrow. The project initially hoped that enough schedules could produce direct cross-occupation wage
comparisons. A rendered challenge exposed weaknesses in assisted wage-table signals, and many documents described pay-setting processes without
supplying comparable wage cells. I did not force those records into a wage estimate. The design expanded to capture compensation mechanisms, staffing
pressure, implementation stages, documentary growth, and bounded local comparisons. That broader framing retained analytically meaningful evidence while
keeping the unavailable national comparison unavailable.

What failed during analysis and publication
The largest analytical failure was not a software error. After reconciliation and strict compatibility checks, the external administrative corpus produced zero
compatible wage matches, zero compatible growth pairs, zero valid vacancy-rate calculations, zero valid overtime-share calculations, and zero valid
total-compensation sums. The corpus was abundant, but abundance was not enough. A valid comparison required more than the same municipality: department
side, period, role, pay basis, compensation basis, and analytical unit had to align. Most records answered a related question rather than that exact one.
I kept those zeros because they were the honest mathematical result. The strict failure also prevented regression. Only three of sixteen regression-readiness
gates passed, so no model was run. Running one anyway would have replaced a missing design with false precision. The project therefore produced no national
wage-gap estimate, no nationally representative growth advantage, no prevalence estimate, and no causal effect.
There were operational interruptions as well. At times the visible interface or coordinator state was interrupted while underlying workers and their checkpointed
outputs remained valid. The response was to inspect process state, accept only completed files, and resume incomplete portions rather than restart the full batch.
A quadratic lookup in one later stage was replaced with indexed access and resume-only-incomplete processing. These repairs affected execution performance,
not evidence interpretation.
Publication also failed independently of research. GitHub Pages runs were delayed, queued, or cancelled without a runner even when the committed PDF was
valid. Direct repository files, local rendering, extracted text, checksums, and raw-file URLs supplied an independent publication check. A deployment failure could

Why I separated strict and broader evidence
As the analytical gates became stricter, they protected the project from overclaiming but also began to place several different kinds of evidence into one unusable
bucket. A locally comparable range, a source-reported percentage, a directional mechanism record, and a genuinely incompatible record do not warrant the same
claim, but neither should they all be discarded for the same reason. I kept the strict baseline and added a layered evidence system on top of it.
Strict evidence contains exact, claim-safe values, formulas, or comparisons. Bounded evidence is analytically usable only with an explicit limitation, such as a
restricted role, period, range, or unit. Directional evidence supports a mechanism or direction but not a precise magnitude. Context helps explain implementation
or setting without serving as claim support. Rejected evidence is incompatible, duplicative, conflicted, unsupported, or otherwise outside the final claim.
This redesign did not relax the wording rules. Broader evidence could strengthen interpretation only within its own limits, and it could also make a claim more
mixed by bringing countervailing records into view. The strict result remained visible in every sensitivity comparison. After the broader review, five claims became
stronger without changing class, one became more mixed, eight were unchanged, and no claim upgraded class. That was useful because it showed that the core
conclusions did not depend on pretending that weaker records were strict proof. It also allowed the atlas to use more of the corpus without forcing a choice
between perfect comparability and ignoring everything else.
The project did not produce the simple national wage-gap answer I originally hoped to estimate. It did produce a large traceable municipal corpus, documented
compensation mechanisms, bounded local comparisons, staffing and implementation evidence, explicit claim boundaries, and a reusable record of the Human-AI
workflow. The main methodological lesson was that scale did not remove the need for judgment. AI made it possible to search, extract, classify, and review
municipal material at a volume I could not have handled manually. As the corpus grew, however, it became more important - not less - to separate successful
processing from analytical proof. The final project records what the evidence could not establish as carefully as what it could.
