INTEREST ARBITRATION OPINION AND AWARD
A Hearing was held on December 7, 2022, in Broadview, Illinois, before Interest Arbitrator Cary
Morgen, having been jointly selected by the Parties, Village of Broadview (“the Employer,” or “the
Village”) and Illinois Fraternal Order of Police Labor Council (“the Union”).

Brian Miller, DEL GADO LAW GROUP, LLC, and testifying for the Village were LeTisa Jones and Chief
Thomas Mills. The Union was represented by its counsel, Jeff Burke, and testifying for the Union was
Bruce Wisniewski. The Parties gave their presentations primarily in narrative fashion. Both Parties were
afforded full opportunity to provide rebuttal testimony as well as to examine and cross-examine witnesses.
A 90-page transcript of the proceedings was prepared. Both Parties filed Post-Hearing Briefs which were
exchanged through the Arbitrator on February 1, 2023, at which time the record was closed.

The Union argues further that wage freezes, as the Village proposes for year 2020-2021, are currently nonexistent in Police bargaining units.
Turning to the internal comparables, there is no evidence that the Police and Fire units have a
history of lockstep bargaining. Significantly, in return for giving the Village a 0% wage increase in 2020,

and a two-person ALS ambulance will cost $100,000 per year if it requires hiring only one firefighter,
which substantially exceeds the Union’s wage proposal as I discuss in more detail later.
Next, the Union considers the cost of living most crucial. Because the first three years of the
Contract have elapsed, increases in the annual cost of living increases are known. The first year, May 2020
– May 2021, the cost of living increased by 5.6%, more than twice the Union’s proposed 2.5%; the Village’s
proposed 0% increase is unsupportable. The second year, May 2021 – May 2022, the cost of living rose
8.8%, almost three times the Union’s proposed 3.0% increase; the Village’s proposed 2% increase simply
cannot be supported. The third year, May 2022 – May 2023, the cost of living increased 1.4% from May to
September 2022; the Philadelphia Federal Reserve revised its annualized forecast to 6.7% up from 4.5%.
The Union’s proposed 3.25% increase for the third year is considerably below the forecast. The Village’s

The evidence shows that the Village is financially stable, and it has not claimed an inability to pay

Based upon the average annual salary of the 14-member bargaining unit, a 1% increase would cost
$850, and roll up to about $1,000 including payroll taxes and pension costs. The Union estimates the total
cost of a 2.5% first-year wage increase to be $32,500, which is less than two- tenths of one percent of the

four, no more than 1.25% separates the Parties’ proposals in each year, which amounts to an added cost of

2.

Health Insurance

The Union proposes the status quo. Currently, a member’s contribution toward the health insurance
premium is 12.5%. Not only is there is no evidence in the record as to the cost of monthly premiums, but
there is also no evidence establishing that the Bargaining Unit members have their health insurance through
the Village. Although arbitrators have increased the percentage contribution paid by employees, it occurs
only when the increase is supported by the evidence, which the Village has not provided. Therefore, the
Arbitrator should adopt the Union’s proposal to maintain the status quo on the health insurance premium.

In addition, the Village had a hybrid Quartermaster/uniform allowance system until March 2, 2022,

4.

New -- Section 31.7 Health Emergencies

The Union proposes adding to Article XXX - General, a new provision “Section 31.7 Health
Emergencies” that states, “The Employer will pay for the employees’ out-of-pocket costs for vaccinations
and/or disease testing it mandates.”
Because of the pandemic health crisis, the Village established policies and directives which
required Police Officers to get frequent tests for COVID which at the time were convenient and free. (Un.
Ex. 9(a-c)) That changed when the free testing facility near Police Headquarters closed, and Officers had
to travel farther on their own time to find another free testing facility. The Union acknowledges the Village
has since rescinded the policies and directives, but argues that in the event it reinstitutes the same or similar
vaccine and/or testing policies, it should be mandated to pay any out-of-pocket costs incurred by Officers
to comply.
Also, the Union insists that since October 12, 2021, it has persistently attempted to get the Village
to bargain over the policy. It was not until the mediation session on October 24, 2022, with the FMCS when
the Union tendered a written proposal to which the Village did not respond. Because the Village chose not
to deal with the matter at Contract negotiations, it is appropriate that it now deal with them through Interest
Arbitration.
Therefore, the Union believes its proposal for the addition of “Section 31.7 Health Emergencies”
is reasonable and should be adopted by the Arbitrator.

DISCUSSION AND FINDINGS
A.

Introduction

Because the issues of wages, health insurance premiums, annual uniform allowance, and a proposed
addition of a new provision regarding testing and vaccines are all economic, my statutory obligation under
Section 14(g) is to “adopt the last offer of settlement which…more nearly complies with the applicable
factors prescribed in subsection (h).” The Act does not require that all factors be addressed, nor does it rank
these factors in terms of their significance; it is my duty to determine which factors bear most heavily in
this particular dispute. See, City of Decatur and IAFF, Local 505, S-MA-86-29 (Eglit, 1986) p. 3. The noneconomic issue whether Officers must provide the Village with receipts of items purchased or maintained
using uniform allowance monies, is left for my discretion.6
The Parties have not given me authority to set Officers’ salaries at rates different from the final
offers, thus I am precluded by statute from fashioning a salary of my choosing. Instead, I must choose
whichever proposal I find most equitable given the totality of circumstances. Arbitrator McAlpin noted that
the term “most equitable” is used because, in Interest Arbitration, equity does not lie exclusively with one
side or the other. See City of Effingham and ILFOP Labor Council, S-MA-07-151 (McAlpin, 2009) p.16;
see also U of I Champaign and ILFOP Labor Council, S-MA-10-76 (McAlpin, 2010) p. 15., and Village
of Oakbrook and Illinois Fraternal Order of Police Labor Council, S-MA-09-017 p. 14 (McAlpin, 2011)
(“In and interest Arbitration the Arbitrator must determine not what the Parties would have agreed to, but
what they should have agreed to.”) (Emphasis added.)
I find this case similar to what Arbitrator Marvin Hill recently faced in Eastern Illinois University
and IL FOP Labor Council, S-MA-23-303 (Hill, 2022). In EIU, the Union’s final offer was a 13.5%
increase over four-years (2022-2025) based primarily on the external comparables and the cost-of-living

Regarding its proposal to require officers to produce receipts for uniform purchases, the Village

In contrast, the Union characterizes a requirement to have officers provide receipts for uniform
purchases as a “breakthrough” proposal, and as such argues that the Arbitrator should apply the established
standard which requires proof that the current system does not work or was creating operational hardships
for the party proposing the change and the opposite party was resisting the change. It adds that the Village
had not provided proof that the old system was broken and that it was suffering operational hardship that
required relief. It also suggests that requiring officers to provide uniform purchase receipts will only serve
to make the current system more cumbersome.
Arbitrator Bierig has held that when a proposal at interest arbitration concerns a relatively minor
change in the status quo it requires a lower burden of proof. Conversely, where the requested change
seeks to create an entirely new or a significant change in a benefit or procedure, Arbitrators apply a
higher standard, the breakthrough analysis, before the Section 14(h) factors are considered. It is well
known that the party seeking to change an existing provision bears the burden of proving a need for the
change. See The Village of Lake Bluff and The Illinois Fraternal Order of Police Labor Council, S-MA16-123, p. 36 (Bierig, 2018).
I have reviewed the record and find that the Village’s proposal that Officers must produce receipts
for a uniform purchase is a minor change. Thus, it requires a lower burden of proof that the change is
justified and does not rise to a level that requires me to apply the seminal three-pronged test established in
Will County Board and Sherriff of Will County, S-MA-88-09, p. 52 (Nathan, 1988). Nevertheless, despite
the lower burden of proof I find the Village has not met its burden. Chief Mills testified that the purpose is
to increase transparency, accountability, and to enable the Village to perform data analysis to determine the
cost of the Contract’s uniform provision. However, the Village presented no evidence to show there had
