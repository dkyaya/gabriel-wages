II.

STANDARD OF REVIEW

5

A complaint must contain “a short and plain statement of the claim showing that the

6

pleader is entitled to relief” such that the defendant is given “fair notice of what

7

the . . . claim is and the grounds upon which it rests.” Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S.

8

545, 555 (2007) (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2); Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41, 47 (1957)).

9

A complaint does not suffice “if it tenders ‘naked assertion[s]’ devoid of ‘further factual

10

enhancement.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S.

11

at 556). Dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) “can be based on the lack of a cognizable legal

12

theory or the absence of sufficient facts alleged under a cognizable legal theory.” Balistreri

13

v. Pacifica Police Dep’t, 901 F.2d 696, 699 (9th Cir. 1988).

14

The Court must accept material allegations in a complaint as true and construe them

15

in the light most favorable to the plaintiff. North Star Int’l v. Arizona Corp. Comm’n, 720

16

F.2d 578, 580 (9th Cir. 1983). “Indeed, factual challenges to a plaintiff’s complaint have

17

no bearing on the legal sufficiency of the allegations under Rule 12(b)(6).” See Lee v. City

18

of Los Angeles, 250 F.3d 668, 688 (9th Cir. 2001).

1.

Monell Claim Against the City

6

In Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978), the Supreme Court

7

held that a municipality generally may not be held liable for a § 1983 violation under a

8

theory of respondeat superior for the actions of its subordinates. A plaintiff can raise a

9

Monell claim against a municipality “(1) when ‘implementation of its official policies or

10

established customs inflicts the constitutional injury,’ (2) ‘for acts of omission, when such

11

omissions amount to the local government’s own official policy,’ or (3) ‘when the

12

individual who committed the constitutional tort was an official with final policy-making

13

authority or such an official ratified a subordinate’s unconstitutional decision or action and

14

the basis for it.’” Andrich v. Kostas, 470 F. Supp. 3d 1048, 1063 (D. Ariz. 2020) (internal

15

citation omitted); see also Clouthier v. Cnty. of Contra Costa, 591 F.3d 1232, 1249–50 (9th

16

Cir. 2010). To successfully plead a Monell claim, allegations in a complaint “may not

17

simply recite the elements of a cause of action but must contain sufficient allegations of

18

underlying facts to give fair notice and to enable the opposing party to defend itself

19

effectively.” AE ex rel. Hernandez v. Cnty. of Tulare, 666 F.3d 631, 637 (9th Cir. 2012)

20

(quoting Starr v. Baca, 652 F.3d 1202, 1207 (9th Cir. 2011)).

21

City. Both theories, however, rely on conclusory allegations.

Official Policy Theory

24

25

26

(3) explain how the policy caused the plaintiff harm; and (4) explain how the policy

27

amounted to deliberate indifference. See Dougherty v. City of Covina, 654 F.3d 892, 900

28

(9th Cir. 2011); see also Sekerke v. City of Nat’l City, No. 19-CV-1360-LAB (MSB), 2020

2

probationary employee’s termination policy. (Doc. 10 ¶ 45.) The policy states that

3

“[d]uring the probationary period, a newly hired or promoted employee may be terminated

4

at any time by the Department Director with the approval of the City Manager, with or

5

without cause and without recourse.” (Id.) Specifically, Mr. Glynn alleges that his

6

termination decision “was made pursuant to the City’s policy and practice of terminating

7

probationary employees ‘with or without cause and without recourse,’ and without regard

8

to whether such termination would otherwise violate a probationary employee’s

constitutional or other guaranteed rights.” (Id. ¶ 57.) But that is where Mr. Glynn’s

10

satisfaction of the Monell standard ends. Other than identifying a policy, Mr. Glynn fails

11

to raise factual allegations to demonstrate (1) the policy’s deficiencies, (2) how those

12

deficiencies were the “moving force” behind his injuries, and (3) that the deficiencies made

13

it obvious that the constitutional injury, his termination, would occur. See Dougherty, 654

14

F.3d at 900.

15

A viable Monell claim must contain factual allegations to support causation between

16

the City’s policy and his purported injury. See City of Los Angeles v. Heller, 475 U.S. 796,

17

799 (1986); see also Sekerke, 2020 WL 4435416, at *12. In Andrich v. Kostas, the plaintiff

18

raised a Monell claim against the City of Phoenix on an official policy theory and identified

19

several police force policies that the City maintained that allegedly inflicted constitutional

20

injuries. 470 F. Supp. 3d at 1064. The court there found the plaintiff’s allegations were

21

conclusory because he failed to raise factual allegations indicating that the policies were

22

the “moving force” behind his injuries. Id.

23

Mr. Glynn’s Monell claim similarly falls short because he relies on conclusory

24

allegations. Mr. Glynn alleges only that his termination decision was made in accordance

25

with the City’s neutral policy “without regard to whether such termination would otherwise

26

violate a probationary employee’s constitutional or other guaranteed rights.”

27

(Doc. 10 ¶¶ 57, 73.) Like the plaintiff in Andrich, Mr. Glynn only asserts a conclusory

28

allegation that the policy resulted in his termination, violating his constitutional rights.

b.

Final Policy-Making Theory

20

Mr. Glynn’s second Monell theory alleges that Fire Chief Long and HR Director

21

Kurek are “final policymakers for City policy with respect to the termination of

22

probationary employees” because they “possess the authority to direct, prepare, and review

23

any necessary department-specific policies for the Fire Department.” (Doc. 10 ¶ 58.)

24

Courts scrutinize a plaintiff’s final policymaker theory because holding a

25

municipality liable for the decisions of officers without final authority is a prohibited

26

application of respondeat superior against a municipality. Pembaur v. City of Cincinnati,

27

475 U.S. 469, 478 (1986). Municipal liability only attaches where “the decisionmaker

28

possesses final authority to establish municipal policy with respect to the action ordered”

-8-

Case 2:23-cv-00612-MTL

Document 17

Filed 10/13/23

Page 9 of 15

1

and “is responsible for establishing final government policy respecting such activity.” Id.

2

at 482. And the fact that an official “has discretion in the exercise of particular functions

3

does not, without more, give rise to municipal liability based on an exercise of that

4

discretion.” Id.

5

Mr. Glynn alleges that Fire Chief Long and Director Kurek have discretion over

6

Fire Department policy. (Doc. 10 ¶ 45.) This, however, is insufficient to establish a basis

7

for municipal liability because Long and Kurek would have to be responsible for

8

establishing the City’s employment policy. See Jett v. Dallas Indep. Sch. Dist., 491 U.S.

9

701, 737 (1989) (holding that district courts must identify official policymakers based on

10

“state and local positive law”) (internal quotations omitted). But responsibility for

11

establishing the City’s employment policy rests with the City Council under

12

El Mirage, Ariz., Code of Ordinances § 30.01-07. The City Code makes clear that the

13

Council has the authority to enact and modify personnel policies: “[t]he Council may adopt,

14

by resolution, rules and regulations to give effect to this subchapter, which may be

15

modified or changed from time to time, but the rules and regulations shall follow the

16

generally accepted principles of good personnel administration.” Id. at § 30.03.

17

Accordingly, Mr. Glynn failed to plead factual allegations to show that Long and Kurek

18

established the City’s employment policy. See City of St. Louis v. Praprotnik, 485 U.S.

19

112, 126 (1988) (holding that “a federal court would not be justified in assuming that

20

municipal policymaking authority lies somewhere other than where the applicable law

21

purports to put it”).

22

In Gillette v. Delmore, the plaintiff, also a firefighter, raised a Monell claim against

23

the City of Eugene, Oregon under the theory that the fire chief was a final policymaker.

24

979 F.2d 1342, 1350 (9th Cir. 1992). The court recognized that the fire chief had authority

25

to hire and fire personnel. Id. The court, however, determined that the fire chief was not a

26

final policymaker after reviewing the Eugene City Charter because he was not responsible

27

for establishing the City’s employment policy. Id. Mr. Glynn’s final policymaker

28

arguments are nearly identical to the plaintiff’s assertions in Gillette. Like in Gillette, this

-9-

Case 2:23-cv-00612-MTL

Court’s review of local law makes clear that Defendants lack authority to make City

2

employment policy. Because the El Mirage Code of Ordinances only grants authority to

3

the City Council to make City employment policy, Long and Kurek are not final

4

policymakers. And because both of Mr. Glynn’s theories to establish Monell liability

against the City fail, the § 1983 claim against the City will be dismissed.

2.

Claims Against Defendants’ Individual Capacities

7

Mr. Glynn alleges that Defendants Dyches, Kurek, and Long violated § 1983 in their

8

individual capacities by depriving him of his constitutional free speech and association

9

rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments. (Doc. 10 ¶¶ 8–10, 48–79.) Defendants

10

argue that Mr. Glynn’s § 1983 claims against the individual defendants are not facially

11

plausible. (Doc. 13 at 15–17.)

12

A claim is facially plausible “when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows

13

the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct

14

alleged.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678. And a court must “draw all reasonable inferences in favor

15

of the nonmoving party.” Boquist v. Courtney, 32 F.4th 764, 773 (9th Cir. 2022).

16

To be individually liable under § 1983, Dyches, Kurek, and Long, must be “integral

17

participant[s]” in the alleged deprivation of constitutional rights. See Boyd v. Benton Cnty.,

18

374 F.3d 773, 780 (9th Cir. 2004). This standard prohibits attaching liability to “mere

19

bystander[s]” who had “no role in the unlawful conduct.” Chuman v. Wright, 76 F.3d 292,

20

294–95 (9th Cir. 1996) (holding that individual liability cannot attach where the

21

deprivation of rights results from a “team effort”). However, “integral participation does

22

not require that each [individual’s] actions themselves rise to the level of a constitutional

23

violation.” Boyd, 374 F.3d at 780. Instead, individuals must have “participated in some

24

meaningful way” for § 1983 liability to attach. Id.

25

A defendant, like Long, Kurek, and Dyches, may be held liable as a supervisor under

26

§ 1983 “if there exists either (1) his or her personal involvement in the constitutional

27

deprivation, or (2) a sufficient causal connection between the supervisor’s wrongful

28

conduct and the constitutional violation.” Starr, 652 F.3d at 1207 (quoting Hansen v. Black,

- 10 -

Case 2:23-cv-00612-MTL

Document 17

Filed 10/13/23

Page 11 of 15

1

885 F.2d 642, 646 (9th Cir. 1989)). And in a § 1983 First Amendment employer retaliation

2

case, “causation can be inferred from timing alone where an adverse employment action

3

follows on the heels of protected activity.” Villiarimo v. Aloha Island Air, Inc., 281 F.3d

4

1054, 1065 (9th Cir. 2002).

5

Drawing all reasonable inferences in Mr. Glynn’s favor, and as further explained

6

below, the Court finds that Mr. Glynn pleads sufficient factual allegations to support “a

7

series of acts” that could establish a “causal connection” between Defendants’ wrongful

8

conduct and the constitutional violations. Starr, 652 F.3d at 1207 (quoting Redman v. Cnty.

9

of San Diego, 942 F.2d 1435, 1447 (9th Cir. 1991)).

10

13

14

employment.” (Id. ¶ 59.) Mr. Glynn also alleges that Dyches was present at the October

15
